Which states make government AI visible to benefits applicants?

When a state agency uses software to score, flag or decide on a Medicaid, SNAP or unemployment case, can the public see that the tool exists, can the person affected learn it was used, is it tested for bias, can a human review the result, and are vendors accountable? This site maps state laws and bills against that five-part framework, plus oversight and enforcement.

Working draft. Most of the details come from secondary sources (law-firm summaries, trackers, LegiScan) and have not been checked against statute text. Each entry shows a confidence level and a source link; the codebook explains how entries are coded.

About this site

The framework

The six rows come from the policy paper this project is built on, which proposes a state bill regulating automated decision systems (ADS) in public benefits programs: (1) a public inventory of ADS; (2) notice and a plain-language explanation to the person affected; (3) impact assessment, testing and monitoring; (4) meaningful human review and appeal of adverse decisions; (5) vendor and developer obligations; plus enforcement and an oversight body. It borrows from OMB memo M-25-21, New York's LOADinG Act, Colorado's SB 26-189 and Texas's TRAIGA.

Codebook

Every entry is coded by hand from its source using the rules below. Codes describe how closely a law or bill resembles each part of the framework in the policy paper, not whether it is good policy.

PartWhat the framework asks forClose match (●)Loose match (◐)
Public inventory
Step 1		Each agency publishes a machine-readable, annually updated inventory of every ADS used in a covered decision: name, purpose, vendor, deployment date, last impact assessment, waivers.Agencies must inventory the AI or ADS they use, with a public posting or report.An inventory exists but is internal or partial, or the law creates a registry of a different kind.
Notice and explanation
Step 2		Each notice says whether an ADS was used, names it, and gives a plain-language explanation of an adverse decision within a set number of days, with ways to correct data and request review.The person affected must be told an automated tool was used, or must receive an explanation of an adverse outcome.Only a general duty to disclose AI use or AI interaction, not tied to a decision about the person.
Impact assessment, testing and monitoring
Step 3		A published assessment before use (accuracy, error rates by protected group, proxy testing, edge cases), ongoing monitoring, re-testing when the model or vendor changes, and discontinuation of discriminatory systems.A required pre-use impact assessment, testing or recurring monitoring of agency systems.Risk-management standards, or an assessment duty that is vague, narrow or known only from a short summary.
Human review and appeal
Step 4		No adverse decision rests solely on automated output. Timely, meaningful human review and an appeal, preserving existing fair-hearing rights.A human must review, verify or make the final decision on decisions that affect rights or benefits.A policy-level requirement, or review limited to certain plans or decision types.
Vendor and developer duties
Step 5		Contracts require documentation of intended uses, limits, validation and subgroup performance, cooperation with audits, tiered disclosure, and no trade-secret bar to applicant disclosure.Developers or vendors must give agencies or deployers documentation, or cooperate with assessments.The vendor is named in an inventory, or the law only touches procurement.
Oversight body and enforcement
Design choice		An independent office or commission, attorney-general enforcement, a private right of action, or none; penalties and cure periods.A designated enforcement authority with penalties, or an independent oversight office.An advisory council or committee, or reporting to the legislature only.

Other codes

  • Rolled back (✕). A duty that was once required and has been removed or repealed. Example: Colorado's SB 26-189 removed impact assessments from the 2024 law.
  • None found (○). Nothing in the sources checked matches this part. It does not prove no such rule exists.
  • Outcome. Enacted: signed into law (the date it takes effect may be later). Repealed before effect: signed, then undone before it applied. Still in session: alive in a legislature that has not adjourned (Massachusetts, Michigan, North Carolina, Pennsylvania and similar). Failed or died: failed a vote, died in committee, was held for study, or was still unpassed when its session ended. Vetoed: passed but vetoed.
  • Scope. Names benefits or eligibility: the text or description explicitly covers benefits, Medicaid, SNAP or eligibility decisions. Government AI use (general): regulates agency AI use without naming benefits.
  • Confidence. High: an official legislature page or two or more independent sources agree. Medium: one secondary source, or sources partly conflict. Low: thin description (often only a title or one summary) and should be checked against the text.
  • Years. Enacted laws count from any year, because the earliest relevant ones (Vermont 2022, Connecticut 2023, New York 2024) predate 2026. Bills are from the 2025 and 2026 sessions.
  • Included. State and federal rules on how agencies use AI or automated decision systems, plus bills and laws on Medicaid, SNAP or eligibility. Not included: chatbot, deepfake, social media, school and private-insurer laws.

Map colors

  • Green: at least one enacted law. Yellow: no enacted law, but a bill is still alive. Red: nothing in force; bills failed, died or were repealed. Gray: nothing found. A state with both an enacted law and failed bills is green.
