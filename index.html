Which states make government AI visible to benefits applicants?

When a state agency uses software to score, flag or decide on a Medicaid, SNAP or unemployment case, can the public see that the tool exists, can the person affected learn it was used, is it tested for bias, can a human review the result, and are vendors accountable? This site maps state laws and bills against that five-part framework, plus oversight and enforcement.

About this site

The framework

The six rows come from the policy paper this project is built on, which proposes a state bill regulating automated decision systems (ADS) in public benefits programs: (1) a public inventory of ADS; (2) notice and a plain-language explanation to the person affected; (3) impact assessment, testing and monitoring; (4) meaningful human review and appeal of adverse decisions; (5) vendor and developer obligations; plus enforcement and an oversight body. It borrows from OMB memo M-25-21, New York's LOADinG Act, Colorado's SB 26-189 and Texas's TRAIGA.

What counts

  • Government use only. The map covers state and federal rules on how agencies use AI or automated decision systems. Chatbot, deepfake, social media, schools and private-insurer laws are not included, even though they were passed in large numbers in 2026. An earlier version of this site counted them; that was the wrong scope for this topic.
  • Benefits-specific entries explicitly name benefits, Medicaid, SNAP or eligibility decisions. Government (general) entries regulate agency AI use without naming benefits.
  • Enacted laws from any year count, because the earliest relevant laws (Vermont 2022, Connecticut 2023, New York 2024) predate 2026. Bills are from the 2025 and 2026 sessions.

Check these before citing

  • New York. The paper describes impact assessments every two years, 30-day advance transmission to the governor, and mandatory discontinuation under the LOADinG Act. Research for this site found that New York's 2025 expansion bill (S 7599, signed Dec. 2025) contained the assessment and inventory article and was repealed in Feb. 2026 by S 8831, before it took effect. I could not confirm whether the original 2024 law (Article 4) contains the assessment duties the paper cites. Please check the statute text.
  • Colorado. SB 26-189 names "essential government services and public benefits," but I could not confirm whether state and local agencies count as regulated deployers.
  • OMB M-25-21. I could not retrieve the memo itself. The benefits and fraud-detection wording comes from your paper.
  • California. AB 302 and SB 896 are described from secondary sources only.
  • Older-law links (such as Arkansas, Montana, Indiana, Texas HB 2060 and the California 2023 and 2024 bills) point to LegiScan's usual address format and were not opened, so a link may not load.
  • Not a complete census. Searches may miss laws in states with thin coverage. A gray state means nothing was found.

Left out on purpose

  • Laws on private insurers' use of AI in prior authorization (Alabama, Utah, Washington, Georgia, Indiana and others). They share goals with benefits transparency but regulate companies, not agencies. Ohio SB 315 and the Medicaid managed-care bills in North Carolina, Michigan and Pennsylvania are kept because they name Medicaid.
  • Federal context that is not a rule: the December 2025 executive order (EO 14365) threatening to preempt state AI laws, the failed congressional moratorium, and MACPAC's 2026 recommendations on human review of automated Medicaid denials.
  • Litigation that motivates the framework: Michigan's MiDAS unemployment fraud system (settled for $20 million), Idaho's Medicaid budget tool (K.W. v. Armstrong) and Arkansas's home-care hours algorithm.
